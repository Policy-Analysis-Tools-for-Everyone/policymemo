Receipt confirmation/ProblemMemoShare···
Plan document, pasted in (condensed)

Problem: applicants sending paper renewals and health declarations receive little or no confirmation that their documents have arrived, leading to calls to the contact centre.

Data: about 1 call in 6 over 3 months was checking whether an application had arrived (~23,000). It can take around 2 weeks from posting to a case being created. First calls spike at around 3 weeks.

using problem

Your problem statement describes the missing fix, not the problem. “Applicants receive little or no confirmation that their documents have been received” defines the problem as the absence of a receipt confirmation, which pre-commits you to sending one, and quietly rules out everything else: closing the two-week gap before a case exists, publishing an expected timescale, telling people whether they may continue in the meantime, or reducing the wait itself.

One thing buried in your own data cuts against the fix as stated: you note it can be around two weeks between posting and case creation. If a confirmation fires on case creation, it arrives at roughly the same moment as the three-week calling spike you’ve identified.

So: if sending a receipt confirmation were off the table entirely, what is the condition you would still be trying to change?

using problem
Problem
Applicants cannot establish whether they may lawfully continue, or when they will know.
Mechanism
No acknowledgement on receipt; roughly two weeks between posting and case creation; no published expected timescale.
Symptom
~23,000 status enquiries, about one call in six.
Constraint
Usable contact details are assumed, not confirmed.

This is a hierarchy, not a single problem. The applicant’s uncertainty and the contact centre’s load sit at different levels, and they do not have the same answers.

Paper renewals: what applicants are waiting to knowFor the service director · Example output

Issue

Applicants who post a renewal cannot find out whether they may lawfully continue while it is assessed, or when they will know. About 1 call in 6 to the contact centre is someone checking their forms arrived.

Analysis

Two things produce those calls. Nothing acknowledges the forms when they arrive, and a case can take around 2 weeks to exist, with no published timescale for a decision. A confirmation sent when the case is created would land at about the same time as the 3-week peak in calls.

Trade-off

Shortening the wait answers both drivers and has the strongest public-value case, but it is the option this team can least easily deliver or authorise. A receipt message is cheap and answers only the first.

Decision needed

Choose which problem this work is for. If it is the contact centre’s load, send the receipt message and say plainly that the wait is set aside. If it is the applicant’s uncertainty, publish a timescale as well. The wait itself is still [add magnitude: time from posting to decision, from case records].

Risk

Some callers may be asking whether they can carry on, not whether their forms arrived. That matters because a receipt message would then move those calls rather than remove them. Checking whether the call sampling separates the two questions would settle it.

What it does

The whole route from a messy concern to a decision someone can read.

There are 9 skills for the analysis and 1 set of house rules that holds across all of them. You don’t choose between them. Each skill comes in when the conversation needs it and steps back when the work moves on. Each skill is built on best-practice methods for policy analysis and evaluation.

  1. Problem

    Pulls the problem apart from the fix you already had in mind, and splits a tangle into the condition, what produces it and what you can see of it.

  2. Stakeholders

    Who holds the problem, who has to fund or sign off a response, and who would be affected differently. Claims about motive need evidence too.

  3. Evidence

    Separates what is known from what is inferred or assumed, and works out which uncertainty actually matters to the decision.

  4. Options

    Builds a real set of choices, so the preferred answer has something to beat besides two decoys and a bigger version of itself.

  5. Criteria

    Settles what counts as better before anything gets scored, and keeps hard constraints apart from the things you are willing to trade.

  6. Outcomes

    Projects what would actually happen, how big and how soon, against what would have happened anyway.

  7. Trade-offs

    Says what each serious option gains and gives up, who carries the loss, and what the choice really turns on.

  8. Decide

    Reaches a recommendation with its strongest counter-argument attached, and the conditions that should reopen it.

  9. Story

    Writes it up for the person who has to read it: a submission, a board paper, an email to a minister. The recommendation goes first.

How it behaves

Built to make weak analysis harder to write.

A general AI model is built to be helpful, and in practice that means producing what you asked for, in the framing you gave it, with plausible material wherever yours runs out. In policy work those are the failures that cost most, because a confident draft built on the wrong problem reads exactly like a good one. The house rules hold in every conversation and change those defaults.

One question at a time

Ask a general model for a problem statement and it writes one. Policymemo.ai asks the question that would change the analysis most, waits for your answer, and drafts once there is something worth writing.

Missing figures stay missing

Where your evidence runs out, a general model tends to fill the gap with a number that sounds right. Policymemo.ai will not make up a figure, a date or a source. It leaves a marked placeholder such as [add magnitude] and names where you would find it.

Competing framings stay visible

Given two readings of a problem, a general model usually picks one and builds on it without saying so. Policymemo.ai shows you both and lets you choose.

Value, capacity and support, together

A general model compares options on whatever you ask it to. Policymemo.ai weighs every option for public value, for whether the money, powers and people exist to deliver it, and for whether the people it depends on will back it, then names what strengthening one costs the others.
